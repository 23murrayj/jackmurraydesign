# Case-study spine

## The problem this is solving

Read the six write-ups back to back and they are six different documents.
Caterpillar is a process story with numbers in it. Sandcastles is a craft
story and the best-written thing on the site. Oasis is a tools list.
Monmouth is a caption. A reader can't compare them, and more importantly
can't tell from the shape of them what you do — they have to read every word
to find out, and most won't.

**The harder thing, said plainly: you have one UX case study.** Caterpillar.
FUSE is adjacent. The other four are graphic design, photography and motion —
good work, but a hiring manager filtering for UX will find one project that
shows UX thinking. The spine below will not fix that. Two things fix it:
writing the Bradley redesign, and making FUSE argue its interface decisions
instead of describing the game. Everything else here is making the existing
work legible.

## The spine

Five beats, same order, every project. Not five headings on the page — the
reader shouldn't see the scaffolding — but every write-up hits them in order.

**1. The ask.** Who wanted what, and the constraint that made it hard. One
sentence. Sandcastles already does this perfectly: *"The brief arrived as
just a couple of sentences for me to build off of."*

**2. The problem under the ask.** What you found once you looked. This is the
beat that separates a designer from someone executing a brief, and it's the
one most often missing. Sandcastles has it: *"Sandcastles is a word about
kids at a beach, and the Psalms are not light reading."* That sentence is
the whole portfolio in miniature.

**3. What I did.** Decisions, not tasks. "Research, wireframes, prototypes"
is a list of nouns anyone can claim. "I chose X over Y because Z" cannot be
faked. Caterpillar currently reads as the noun list; it has the receipts to
do better.

**4. The hinge.** The one decision the project turned on, and what you
rejected. One per project, no more. This is what people remember, and it's
the thing an interviewer will ask you to expand on — so pick the one you
most want to be asked about.

**5. What happened.** Outcome, as evidence rather than adjective. "Drew a
strong response" is an adjective. *"Best Research Award"* is evidence.

## One structural note

The spine is prose, not headings. Resist "Problem / Process / Outcome"
subheads: they make every project look like a template, which is the thing
that makes portfolios forgettable. The reader should feel the structure
without being shown it. The current write-ups are already paragraph-shaped;
keep that and fix what the paragraphs contain.
