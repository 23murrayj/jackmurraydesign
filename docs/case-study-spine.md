# Case-study spine

## The problem this is solving

Read the six write-ups back to back and they are six different documents.
Caterpillar is a process story with numbers in it. Sandcastles is a craft
story and the best-written thing on the site. Oasis is a tools list.
Monmouth is a caption. A reader can't compare them, and more importantly
can't tell from the shape of them what you do — they have to read every word
to find out, and most won't.

**The harder thing, said plainly: you have one UX case study.** Caterpillar.
FUSE is adjacent. The other four are graphic design, photography and motion —
good work, but a hiring manager filtering for UX will find one project that
shows UX thinking. The spine below will not fix that. Two things fix it:
writing the Bradley redesign, and making FUSE argue its interface decisions
instead of describing the game. Everything else here is making the existing
work legible.

## The spine

Five beats, same order, every project. Not five headings on the page — the
reader shouldn't see the scaffolding — but every write-up hits them in order.

**1. The ask.** Who wanted what, and the constraint that made it hard. One
sentence. Sandcastles already does this perfectly: *"The brief arrived as
just a couple of sentences for me to build off of."*

**2. The problem under the ask.** What you found once you looked. This is the
beat that separates a designer from someone executing a brief, and it's the
one most often missing. Sandcastles has it: *"Sandcastles is a word about
kids at a beach, and the Psalms are not light reading."* That sentence is
the whole portfolio in miniature.

**3. What I did.** Decisions, not tasks. "Research, wireframes, prototypes"
is a list of nouns anyone can claim. "I chose X over Y because Z" cannot be
faked. Caterpillar currently reads as the noun list; it has the receipts to
do better.

**4. The hinge.** The one decision the project turned on, and what you
rejected. One per project, no more. This is what people remember, and it's
the thing an interviewer will ask you to expand on — so pick the one you
most want to be asked about.

**5. What happened.** Outcome, as evidence rather than adjective. "Drew a
strong response" is an adjective. *"Best Research Award"* is evidence.

## Per project

### Caterpillar UX Challenge
Has the most evidence and uses it worst. Best Research Award, 5 usability
participants, 7 weeks with Caterpillar's UX team, 2 rounds of iteration, an
on-site visit — all present, all in a list.

- **Missing: beat 2.** What did the research actually find about heavy
  equipment operators that you didn't know going in? That finding is why
  there was an award.
- **Missing: beat 4.** What did round 2 change, and why? "2 rounds of
  iteration" is a process fact; "testing killed X so we did Y" is a design fact.
- Still blocked on the confidentiality email, so this stays words-only for now.

### FUSE — Partners in Crime
The strongest *premise* on the site and it's described rather than argued.
"The two players never see the same thing" is a genuinely hard interface
problem — asymmetric information, two input models, one shared state.

- **Missing: beat 2 and 4, entirely.** What breaks when one player is in a
  headset and the other is reading a floor plan? What did you build to fix it?
- **Beat 5 exists and is underplayed:** it was shown publicly at the Peoria
  Riverfront Museum. Did you watch people play it? What did they get stuck
  on? That's user research whether or not it was called that.

### Sandcastles
Closest to the spine already. Beats 1, 2, 3 and 4 are all there — the hinge
is the creative director swapping brush script for serif capitals, and the
"how much each one says depends on how long anyone looks at it" paragraph is
better systems thinking than most junior portfolios contain.

- **Missing: beat 5 only.** Fifteen weeks, every Sunday, a full auditorium.
  Anything you can say about how it held up over that run.

### Easter at Riverside
Beat 1 and 3 are there. The blue matched to the church's worship room wall
is a hinge hiding in a subordinate clause — promote it.

- **Missing: beat 2.** One weekend, two services, two different moods
  (Good Friday and Easter Sunday). One identity across both. *That* is the
  problem, and it's a real one.
- **Missing: beat 5.** Attendance, reach, or simply that it ran at the
  building scale it was designed for.

### Monmouth Retreat
A photography collection, and the spine should flex hardest here — forcing
"problem/solution" onto candid photography would read as fake.

- Keep beats 1, 2 and 4. Beat 2 is already there in one line: the changing
  light. Beat 4 is the editing decision you made because of it.
- **Beat 5 is the honest gap:** what were the photos *for*? Who used them?

### Journey to the Oasis
Currently the tools list — Maya, Blender, ZBrush, Cycles over Eevee, three
days to render. That Cycles-over-Eevee call is a real beat 4 with a stated
tradeoff; it's just stranded among software names.

- **Missing: beat 1 and 2.** Why this story? A course brief is still an ask.
- **Beat 5 is an adjective** — "drew a strong response." Either make it
  evidence or cut it; an unsupported claim reads weaker than no claim.

## One structural note

The spine is prose, not headings. Resist "Problem / Process / Outcome"
subheads: they make every project look like a template, which is the thing
that makes portfolios forgettable. The reader should feel the structure
without being shown it. The current write-ups are already paragraph-shaped;
keep that and fix what the paragraphs contain.
